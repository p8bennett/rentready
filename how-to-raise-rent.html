How to Raise Rent Legally: A Landlord's Step-by-Step Guide

By RentReady · Rent Management Guide

Raising rent is one of the most important — and most mishandled — things a landlord does. Do it wrong and you risk losing a good tenant, violating state law, or both. Do it right and it's a routine part of running a healthy rental business. This guide walks through every step of the process.

When Can a Landlord Raise Rent?

Fixed-term leases

If your tenant is on a 12-month lease, you generally cannot raise the rent until the lease expires — unless the lease itself includes a clause allowing for mid-term increases. Most standard leases do not include this. The appropriate time to implement an increase is at lease renewal, with proper written notice delivered before the current lease ends.

Month-to-month tenancies

Month-to-month tenants can have their rent raised at any time with proper written notice. Most states require 30 days notice; some require 60 or even 90 days for increases above a certain threshold. Always check your state's specific requirement before sending notice.

Rent-controlled properties

If your property is subject to rent control or rent stabilization — common in cities like New York, San Francisco, Los Angeles, and some parts of New Jersey and Massachusetts — increases are limited to a set percentage or formula determined by the local rent board. Operating a rent-controlled unit without knowing the local rules is a serious liability.

How Much Can You Raise Rent?

In most states and cities with no rent control, there is no legal cap on how much you can increase rent. The limit is a practical one — raise it too high and your tenant leaves, costing you 1–2 months of lost rent plus turnover costs.

As a general benchmark, most experienced landlords raise rent annually in the 3–5% range. This keeps pace with inflation and rising property costs while minimizing tenant turnover. Larger increases — 10–15% or more — tend to trigger tenant searches for alternatives even when the market rate would support it.

CPI-based increases

Tying increases to the Consumer Price Index (CPI) is the most defensible approach, particularly in tenant-friendly markets. The CPI is published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics at bls.gov/cpi. When a tenant asks why you're raising rent, "because inflation increased 3.8% this year" is a harder argument to dispute than an arbitrary number.

Step-by-Step: How to Raise Rent the Right Way

1

Check your lease and local laws

Confirm your tenant's lease type (fixed-term vs. month-to-month), look up your state's notice requirement, and verify whether your city or county has any rent control or stabilization rules that cap the increase amount.

2

Calculate your new rent

Decide on your increase percentage based on local market rents, your expenses, and CPI if applicable. Use our rent increase calculator to see the exact new monthly amount and the annual impact.

3

Deliver written notice on time

Send the notice with enough lead time to satisfy your state's requirement — and then some. A notice postmarked on the 30th day in a 30-day-notice state is cutting it too close. Give yourself a buffer and send it early.

4

Keep a copy of everything

Save a copy of the notice, the delivery method (email, certified mail, personal delivery), and the date it was sent. If a dispute ever arises about when the tenant was notified, documentation is your only protection.

5

Update the lease at renewal

Don't just raise the rent verbally. Ensure the new amount is reflected in the renewed or updated lease agreement signed by both parties. A verbal rent increase with no updated lease creates ambiguity.

Turnover math: If your tenant pays $2,000/month and you raise rent $200 to push the market rate, but they leave — you lose $4,000 in vacancy, spend $500 cleaning, and spend another $300 advertising. That $200 increase needed 25 months to break even. Know your numbers before being aggressive.

How to Write the Notice

A rent increase notice doesn't need to be a legal document, but it does need to include the key information clearly: your name and contact information, the tenant's name and unit address, the current rent amount, the new rent amount, the date the increase takes effect, and the date the notice was delivered. Keep the tone professional and matter-of-fact. You don't need to justify the increase, but a brief acknowledgment — "in response to increased operating costs" — can soften the reception.

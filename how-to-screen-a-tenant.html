How to Screen a Tenant: What to Check Before Signing a Lease

By RentReady · Tenant Screening Guide

Choosing the wrong tenant is the single most expensive mistake a landlord can make. A bad eviction costs $3,000–$10,000 in legal fees, lost rent, and repairs — and takes months. A thorough screening process, applied consistently to every applicant, is your best protection. Here's how to do it right.

Set Your Criteria Before You Start

The most important step in tenant screening happens before you show the unit. Write down your minimum criteria and apply them consistently to every applicant. This protects you legally under Fair Housing law — you're rejecting based on objective criteria, not subjective impressions.

Typical minimum criteria include:

  • Income: Gross monthly income at least 2.5–3× the monthly rent
  • Credit score: Minimum threshold (620–650 is common for independent landlords)
  • Rental history: No evictions in the past 5 years
  • Criminal history: Based on local laws — some jurisdictions restrict how this can be used
  • Employment: Stable employment or verifiable income source

Fair Housing reminder: Federal law prohibits discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability. Many states and cities add additional protected classes. Apply the same screening criteria to every applicant and document your decisions.

The Five-Step Screening Process

1. Pre-screen on the phone or by message

Before showing the unit, ask a few basic questions: When are you looking to move? How many people will be living in the unit? Do you have pets? What is your monthly income? This filters out applicants who are clearly not a fit before you invest time in a showing.

2. Collect a written application

Every serious applicant should complete a rental application that includes full legal name, current and previous addresses, employment information and income, references, and authorization to run a credit and background check. Never skip the written application — it's your legal basis for the screening decisions you make.

3. Verify income

Don't take an applicant's word for their income. Ask for the two most recent pay stubs, or for self-employed applicants, two years of tax returns. Bank statements for the past two months are also useful. The 3× income rule is a reasonable standard — a tenant paying more than one-third of their gross income in rent is at higher risk of payment issues.

4. Run a credit and background check

A credit check reveals payment history, outstanding debt, and any prior eviction judgments. A background check surfaces criminal history. Services like TransUnion SmartMove allow landlords to request that applicants pay for their own report — which is both cost-effective and common practice.

5. Call previous landlords

This is the step most landlords skip — and the one that tells you the most. Call the previous landlord listed on the application and ask: Did this tenant pay rent on time? Would you rent to them again? Did they give proper notice before moving? Did they leave the unit in good condition? A landlord's hesitation or vague answers tells you as much as what they actually say.

Pro tip: Call the landlord before the one listed. A current landlord who wants a difficult tenant to leave has incentive to give a positive reference. The previous landlord has nothing to gain either way.

Tenant Screening Checklist

Before Showing

Written screening criteria defined and documented
Phone pre-screen completed
Application form ready to send or collect

Application Review

Written application received and signed
Income verified with pay stubs or tax returns
Employment confirmed (called employer directly)
Credit report reviewed
Background check reviewed
Eviction history checked
Previous landlord called (the one before the current one)
Decision documented with reason

What to Do When You Reject an Applicant

When you reject an applicant based on a credit or background report, federal law (the Fair Credit Reporting Act) requires you to send an adverse action notice. This notice must explain that you used a consumer report in your decision, name the reporting company, and advise the applicant of their right to get a free copy of their report. Screening services like SmartMove automate this process for you.

Keep a file on every applicant — accepted or rejected — for at least three years. If a Fair Housing complaint is ever filed against you, documentation of your consistent, criteria-based process is your defense.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Reluctance to provide references or authorize a credit check
  • Income that can't be verified with documentation
  • Gaps in rental history with vague explanations
  • Wanting to move in immediately with a long-term lease
  • Paying the deposit in cash without a clear reason
  • A previous landlord who says they'd "prefer not to comment"

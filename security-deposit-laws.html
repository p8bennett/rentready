Security Deposit Laws by State: The Complete Landlord Guide

By RentReady · State Law Reference

Security deposit rules are entirely state-governed, which means the rules in Massachusetts are completely different from the rules in Texas. Getting this wrong — even accidentally — can cost you the right to keep any portion of the deposit and expose you to double or triple damages in some states. This guide covers the key rules for 12 states in plain English.

The Three Rules Every Landlord Must Know

Regardless of your state, three rules apply almost universally. Miss any one of them and you risk losing your deposit rights entirely.

  • Charge only what's legal. Many states cap the deposit at one or two months' rent. Charging more than the legal maximum is a violation even if the tenant agrees to it in writing.
  • Return it on time. Every state has a strict deadline — ranging from 14 days to 45 days after move-out. Missing it by even one day in some states eliminates your right to make any deductions.
  • Provide an itemized statement. Most states require a written list of any deductions. A vague "cleaning fee" won't hold up. Specificity — "carpet cleaning, unit 2B, $180" — is what the law requires.

State-by-State Reference

Massachusetts

Maximum deposit1× monthly rent
Return deadline30 days after move-out
Interest requiredYes — 5% annually or bank rate
StatuteMass. Gen. Laws ch. 186 § 15B

Massachusetts has some of the strictest deposit laws in the country. The deposit must be held in a separate, interest-bearing Massachusetts bank account. Interest must be paid to the tenant annually or credited toward rent. Failure to comply gives the tenant the right to deduct the interest directly from rent — and willful violations can result in damages of three months' rent plus attorney fees.

Connecticut

Maximum deposit2× monthly rent (under 62) / 1× (age 62+)
Return deadline30 days after move-out
Interest requiredYes — average savings deposit rate
StatuteConn. Gen. Stat. § 47a-21

Connecticut's age-based rule is unique — tenants 62 or older are protected by a lower cap. Interest accrues annually and must be paid to the tenant each year or applied as a rent credit. If the landlord fails to pay interest, the tenant may legally deduct it from rent.

New York

Maximum deposit1× monthly rent
Return deadline14 days after move-out
Interest requiredYes — buildings with 6+ units
StatuteN.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108

New York's 14-day return deadline is one of the shortest in the country. Landlords must provide an itemized statement of deductions within that window or forfeit the right to keep any portion of the deposit.

California

Maximum deposit2× monthly rent (unfurnished)
Return deadline21 days after move-out
Interest requiredNo
StatuteCal. Civ. Code § 1950.5

Furnished units are capped at 3× monthly rent. California requires receipts for any deduction over $125. Bad faith retention can result in up to 2× the deposit in additional damages.

Florida

Maximum depositNo statutory maximum
Return deadline15 days (no deductions) / 30 days (with deductions)
Interest requiredNo
StatuteFla. Stat. § 83.49

Florida has two separate deadlines depending on whether you're making deductions. If you intend to keep any portion, you must send written notice within 30 days. The deposit must be held in a separate Florida bank account.

Texas

Maximum depositNo statutory maximum
Return deadline30 days after move-out
Interest requiredNo
StatuteTex. Prop. Code § 92.101–92.109

Texas is landlord-friendly compared to most states — no deposit cap, no interest requirement. However, bad faith retention is expensive: courts can award 3× the deposit amount plus $100 plus attorney fees.

Arizona

Maximum deposit1.5× monthly rent
Return deadline14 days after move-out
Interest requiredNo
StatuteAriz. Rev. Stat. § 33-1321

Arizona's 14-day return deadline requires prompt action. Non-refundable fees (cleaning fees, pet fees) must be explicitly labeled as non-refundable in the lease — otherwise they're treated as part of the deposit.

The Most Expensive Landlord Mistakes

Based on common landlord-tenant disputes, these are the mistakes that cost landlords the most money:

  • Missing the return deadline — even by one day — often eliminates the right to make any deductions
  • Vague deduction descriptions — "damages" isn't sufficient; you need itemized line items with dollar amounts
  • No move-in inspection — without a signed move-in checklist, proving pre-existing damage is nearly impossible
  • Charging for normal wear and tear — nail holes, minor scuffs, and carpet fading are not deductible in any state
  • Mixing deposit money with personal funds — required to be separate in many states

Best practice: Do a walk-through with the tenant at move-in and move-out. Have them sign a dated inspection checklist with photos attached. That documentation is your only protection in a dispute.

Rental Property Tax Deductions: The Schedule E Guide for Landlords

By RentReady · Tax Planning Guide

Schedule E is the IRS form where landlords report rental income and expenses. It's also where most independent landlords either save significant money — or leave it on the table because they didn't know what was deductible. This guide covers everything you can legally deduct as a rental property owner and how to stay organized throughout the year so tax season isn't a scramble.

This is general tax information, not tax advice. Your specific situation may differ. Always consult a CPA or tax professional who works with real estate investors before filing.

What Is Schedule E?

Schedule E (Supplemental Income and Loss) is the IRS form attached to your personal Form 1040 that reports income and expenses from rental properties. Each property gets its own column on Schedule E, and the net result — profit or loss — flows to your personal income tax return.

Rental income is generally considered passive income, which means losses are typically limited to $25,000 per year (and only if your adjusted gross income is under $100,000). This passive activity loss rule is one of the most misunderstood aspects of rental property taxation — a good reason to have a CPA on your side.

What Landlords Can Deduct on Schedule E

🏠

Mortgage Interest

The interest portion of your mortgage payment — typically the largest deduction for leveraged landlords.

📋

Property Taxes

Real estate taxes paid to your state or local government during the tax year.

🛡️

Landlord Insurance

Premiums for your rental property insurance policy. Homeowner's insurance on your primary residence is not deductible here.

🔧

Repairs & Maintenance

Fixing a leaky faucet, repainting, replacing a broken window. Repairs are fully deductible in the year incurred.

👷

Property Management

Fees paid to a property manager or management company, typically 8–12% of collected rent.

📢

Advertising

Listing fees, photography, signage, and any other costs to market a vacant unit.

⚖️

Professional Fees

Attorney fees for lease drafting or eviction, CPA fees for rental tax preparation, and similar professional services.

🚗

Travel & Mileage

Mileage driven to the property for repairs, inspections, or tenant meetings. Keep a mileage log.

Repairs vs. Improvements: A Critical Distinction

This is the single most common point of confusion in rental property taxes. The IRS treats repairs and improvements very differently.

Repairs restore the property to its original working condition. They're fully deductible in the year you pay for them. Fixing a broken HVAC unit, patching a roof leak, replacing a broken door — these are repairs.

Improvements add value or extend the useful life of the property. They must be depreciated over time rather than deducted all at once. Adding a new HVAC system where there wasn't one, replacing an entire roof, renovating a kitchen — these are improvements.

In practice, the line can be blurry. A CPA familiar with real estate can help you categorize expenses correctly and potentially use Section 179 or bonus depreciation to accelerate deductions on some improvements.

Depreciation: The Silent Deduction

Depreciation is often the largest deduction a landlord takes — and the one most beginners don't claim correctly. The IRS assumes residential rental property wears out over 27.5 years. That means you can deduct 1/27.5th of the building's value (not the land) each year, regardless of whether the property actually declines in value.

On a $300,000 property where $250,000 is allocated to the structure (not land), your annual depreciation deduction is approximately $9,090 — completely reducing taxable income without any cash outlay.

Important: When you sell the property, the IRS "recaptures" depreciation deductions at a special 25% rate. This is called depreciation recapture and is a key reason to work with a tax professional when selling a rental property.

How to Stay Organized All Year

The landlords who pay the least in taxes are the ones who track expenses consistently — not the ones who scramble every April. A simple system goes a long way:

  • Separate bank account — run all rental income and expenses through a dedicated account
  • Track every expense at the time it occurs — not three months later from memory
  • Save every receipt — even small ones; they add up and they protect you in an audit
  • Log every mile driven for property-related purposes
  • Categorize expenses monthly — don't let 12 months pile up in January

The goal is a clean income and expense summary per property that you can hand to your CPA at year-end. The less time they spend organizing your records, the less you pay them — and the fewer deductions get missed.
